GOOD LOCAL TEST LAB — NOT YET PROVEN WITH REAL CUSTOMERS

Run date: 29 August 2026 · Evidence level: fresh local runs on synthetic, saved, or controlled data; no production customer traffic

The short answer

You have built a good support QA test lab.

It can catch many bad support behaviours before release. It is not yet a proven live QA system because you do not have real customer traffic or a live support operation. That is an evidence gap, not a personal failure.

What I found

What you built

A repeatable way to give a support AI a customer problem, watch what it does, check the result, and save the proof.

What works well

Your tests are good at catching wrong routing, early guessing, unsafe promises, actions that were never confirmed, and weak handoffs.

What is weak

The intent classifier struggles with normal customer language. The AI quality reviewers still miss some bad answers. The combined product proof is out of date.

What you can honestly say

You built and tested a support-evaluation method across chat, technical support, handoffs, voice, screen guidance, and support operations. Do not call it production-proven yet.

What I would do next

  1. Keep the framework. The basic design is sound.
  2. Add 25 to 50 naturally worded customer cases, especially for technical help and account access. You do not need a live employer or real customer data to do this.
  3. Connect one of your non-live support products to the framework and show one clean report containing normal cases, hard cases, and deliberate failures.

These are different kinds of tests, so they do not form one pass rate.

  • The shared framework passed all 44 of its own tests, passed five normal chat journeys, and blocked all 12 deliberate chat and voice defects.
  • The strongest common pattern is evidence-timed support: the system should ask or act only when the case record supports that step, then verify what changed before it claims success.
  • The intent classifier is the sharpest quality warning. Its synthetic accuracy is about 90–96%, but the five tested model versions scored only 20–50% on ten naturally worded customer messages.
  • The local QA judges reached 38/40 calibrated accuracy, but one real unsafe promise still passed and three responses did not match the required JSON format.
  • Voice Support handled all 11 saved journeys correctly in deterministic mode, but its release verdict stayed blocked because the five AI QA reviewers did not run. One speech-worker restart test timed out in the full suite, then passed on a focused retry.
  • Support Binder's 82 tests passed, but its cross-product seal rejected the run because several checked-out components no longer match the recorded proof commits and tags.
  • No result here comes from real customer traffic. That is a limit on the claim, not proof that the framework cannot be used by a live support team.

How the reusable framework behaved

What this run cannot claim

  • No real customers, production tickets, production calls, CSAT, first-contact resolution, or business outcome was measured.
  • Paid OpenRouter, Anthropic, and provider-based model runs were not started when a required key was absent or the run would spend money. They remain visible as not run.
  • Live Zammad acceptance was not repeated because it changes helpdesk records. The current run stayed read-only outside Support Evals.
  • Full-screen provider evaluation and live telephony were not run. Captured traces and controlled app fixtures were used instead.
  • Scores from unit tests, model comparisons, customer journeys, benchmark arms, and attack cases answer different questions. They must not be merged into one portfolio pass rate.
  • A local pass means the declared check passed in this environment. It does not mean the same system will pass with real customer language, policy changes, broken integrations, or production load.
