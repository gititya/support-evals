A lesson from the work, not a tour of the repos

What I learned from building support products

The useful result was not a bigger support bot. It was better judgment about when a system may understand, diagnose, act, promise, hand off, or stop.

The strongest lesson A support decision is only as good as the evidence available when it is made.
Claim boundary

This lesson draws on local tests, synthetic cases, saved traces, public complaints, controlled product states, and supervised local app runs. It does not claim production customer outcomes.

The problem I kept returning to

Support can look complete before the customer is safe

A reply can sound clear, a route can look sensible, an action can be recorded, and an escalation can be sent. Each can still fail the customer.

“A reply is evidence of communication. It is not evidence of resolution.”

Working rule from Support Evals
1

The system hears a symptom and mistakes it for a cause.

2

It chooses a plausible path before checking what changed.

3

It says “done,” “safe,” or “escalated” before the outcome exists.

4

The customer repeats work, enters the wrong queue, or trusts a false promise.

What changed my mind

Speed looked like the advantage

The realtime experiment asked whether the exact cause could be named from the opening turns. On stronger synthetic calls, the early result fell to 2% against a 60% build gate, while the full call reached 92%. The test worked; the cause was not available early.

Kill early root-cause prediction. Keep the pre-build gate.

The strongest cross-product lessons

I used to think / I learned

These are the beliefs the evidence changed. Each one points to a different product decision.

I used to think speed meant knowing the answer sooner.

Speed means reaching the next justified check sooner.

Early certainty can increase customer effort when it sends the case down the wrong path.

I used to think a clearer transcript was safer.

Preserved meaning matters more than polished wording.

A correction or negation is operational data. Cleanup that changes it is not an edit; it is a support error.

I used to think more rules made an AI reviewer stricter.

A reviewer must earn trust through calibration.

More prompt detail can create more ways to excuse a bad reply. Compare the reviewer with approved human judgments.

I used to think the final reply proved the outcome.

The product state proves whether the action worked.

“I changed it” is a claim. A fresh account, order, ticket, or screen state is evidence.

I used to think escalation ended the automated journey.

A handoff succeeds only when the next owner can continue.

Correct routing with a thin note still makes the customer repeat the case.

I used to think a large synthetic test set created realism.

Test-data provenance can change the conclusion.

A stronger call author erased the realtime experiment's promising early signal. The author was part of the system under test.

The reusable method

Evidence-gated support decisions

This is the method that survived the individual products. It is support QA expressed as a sequence of defendable decisions.

Name the customer effect first

Define the harm in support terms: repeat contact, wrong queue, unsafe promise, unverified action, or thin handoff.

State the load-bearing assumption

Ask what must be true for the product idea to help. Set the build gate before seeing the result.

Separate what is known from what is guessed

Preserve corrections and open questions. Do not let a plausible branch become a final answer without mechanism evidence.

Use the right kind of reviewer

Use fixed rules for facts and permissions, AI reviewers for bounded judgment, and people to calibrate the reviewers and discover missing criteria.

Verify action and final state

Check that the action ran, the expected state exists, and the observation is fresh. A sentence cannot verify itself.

Bind the claim to its proof

Record the exact cases, source, version, failed runs, and limits. Do not merge local, synthetic, controlled, and production evidence.

Only say or do what the available evidence at that point in the customer journey supports.

Concrete product decisions

Build, adapt, or kill

The same evidence can justify three different moves. Select a decision to see examples from the work.

Build when the method survives the test

Build the evidence boundary

The strongest parts are the ones that make support claims harder to fake and easier to inspect.

  • Evidence-timed case state: facts, unknowns, branches, next check, and justified resolution.
  • Deterministic risk floors, bounded troubleshooting, and fail-closed handoff.
  • Action and fresh-state verification, including customer-only screen guidance.
  • A reusable local harness that keeps errors, unsafe outcomes, and abstentions visible.

Make the claim boundary visible

What is true, promising, and unknown

The evidence is useful because the limit stays attached to the claim.

Verified now

  • The local harness catches the deliberate support failures represented in its fictional chat and saved voice cases.
  • Several source systems enforce evidence timing, risk floors, handoff fields, fresh-state checks, and proof-version rules in controlled environments.
  • Some initial beliefs failed: early exact-cause prediction, default transcript cleanup, synthetic-only intent confidence, and uncalibrated reviewer trust.

Reasonable inference

  • The cross-product method is coherent: require enough evidence before each support decision, then verify the outcome.
  • The best first operational use is likely offline or shadow QA, because it can compare system judgments with human review without affecting customers.
  • The reusable harness can accept more products through adapters, but each adapter still needs to be built and tested.

Not yet known

  • Whether the method improves customer effort, repeat contact, CSAT, resolution, or safety in production.
  • How it behaves across real language, accents, policies, product changes, broken integrations, and production load.
  • How often human QA will disagree, override, or discover missing checks once real cases enter the loop.

Plain-English glossary

Terms worth keeping

Evidence gate

A rule that stops a decision until the case contains enough proof to defend it.

False-safe

A dangerous case that the system wrongly passes as safe.

Deterministic check

A fixed rule with the same result for the same input; no AI judgment is involved.

Calibration

Comparing a reviewer with approved human judgments and measuring where it disagrees.

Abstention

The reviewer says it lacks enough evidence to judge instead of inventing a pass or fail.

Drift

The cases, policy, customer behavior, or reviewer change until an old result stops matching reality.

Handoff contract

The minimum facts, evidence, unknowns, owner, and next action required before a transfer.

Fresh state

A new observation made after the action, not the same screen or record seen before it.

Proof seal

A record that binds a result to the exact code and evidence versions that produced it.
